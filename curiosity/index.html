    Distributed Systems Curiosities

    Distributed systems are memorable because apparently reasonable assumptions fail in surprising ways. Each curiosity below is tied to a lesson and includes a small thought experiment or reproducible activity.

    Warm-up

    Section titled “Warm-up”

    Warm-up — Laboratory Bootcamp

    Section titled “Warm-up — Laboratory Bootcamp”

    Three processes on one laptop are still a distributed experiment—but not three hardware failure domains

    Section titled “Three processes on one laptop are still a distributed experiment—but not three hardware failure domains”

    Separate processes can communicate only through explicit interfaces and can fail independently at the process level, which is enough to study many distributed semantics. They still share one kernel, disk, power source, and often one network stack.

    Try it: Run three service processes, kill only one, then list which failures would still take down all three simultaneously.

    Part I — Foundations and Architecture

    Section titled “Part I — Foundations and Architecture”

    Chapter 1 — Thinking in Distributed Systems

    Section titled “Chapter 1 — Thinking in Distributed Systems”

    A timeout tells you what you did not observe, not what happened remotely

    Section titled “A timeout tells you what you did not observe, not what happened remotely”

    The request may have been lost, delayed, executed, committed, or completed while only the reply disappeared. Distributed systems become difficult exactly because local absence of evidence does not identify the remote state.

    Try it: Draw four executions that all end with the client seeing the same timeout but leave the server in four different states.

    Chapter 2 — Architectures and Middleware

    Section titled “Chapter 2 — Architectures and Middleware”

    One line of Ray can imply scheduling, serialization, data movement, process selection, and failure policy

    Section titled “One line of Ray can imply scheduling, serialization, data movement, process selection, and failure policy”

    A remote task call is intentionally compact syntax. The middleware’s value is that it hides a large distributed control path; the course uses that convenience first and then asks you to identify every hidden dependency before Ray is removed.

    Try it: For one f.remote(x) call, write the minimum sequence of hidden steps that must occur before the caller can obtain the result.

    Chapter 3 — Processes, Virtualization, and Distributed Execution

    Section titled “Chapter 3 — Processes, Virtualization, and Distributed Execution”

    A distributed protocol can be correct on paper and still fail because one node has a local race

    Section titled “A distributed protocol can be correct on paper and still fail because one node has a local race”

    Duplicate suppression, leader state, membership, and queues are implemented by ordinary concurrent programs. A bad check-then-act interleaving inside one process can violate a distributed invariant before the network does anything unusual.

    Try it: Construct two concurrent handlers that both observe a missing request ID and then both apply the same effect.

    Part II — Communication and Coordination

    Section titled “Part II — Communication and Coordination”

    Chapter 4 — Communication I — Messages, RPC, and Remote Failure

    Section titled “Chapter 4 — Communication I — Messages, RPC, and Remote Failure”

    A retry can make a reliable client less correct

    Section titled “A retry can make a reliable client less correct”

    If the first remote operation succeeded but its reply was lost, an automatic retry can duplicate a payment, append, email, or state transition. Reliability at the transport/request level and correctness at the application-effect level are different properties.

    Try it: Take one non-idempotent API operation and redesign it around a stable request ID plus durable duplicate suppression.

    Chapter 5 — Communication II — Messaging, Dissemination, and Overlays

    Section titled “Chapter 5 — Communication II — Messaging, Dissemination, and Overlays”

    Gossip works partly because nobody has to know the exact same membership view at the exact same time

    Section titled “Gossip works partly because nobody has to know the exact same membership view at the exact same time”

    Epidemic dissemination accepts temporary disagreement and uses repeated peer exchange to spread information. This weak synchronization can scale better than requiring one central, immediately authoritative membership table for every change.

    Try it: Simulate one membership update spreading through eight nodes when each informed node contacts one random peer per round.

    Chapter 6 — Coordination I — Time, Events, and Causality

    Section titled “Chapter 6 — Coordination I — Time, Events, and Causality”

    A log can show a reply before the request without time travel

    Section titled “A log can show a reply before the request without time travel”

    Wall clocks on different machines can disagree enough that timestamp sorting contradicts message causality. Message identity and happened-before can prove the causal direction even when displayed clock readings suggest the opposite.

    Try it: Create two artificial log clock offsets and produce a send/receive pair whose wall timestamps appear reversed.

    Chapter 7 — Coordination II — Mutual Exclusion, Elections, and Agreement

    Section titled “Chapter 7 — Coordination II — Mutual Exclusion, Elections, and Agreement”

    Two processes can both believe they are leader without two conflicting values becoming committed

    Section titled “Two processes can both believe they are leader without two conflicting values becoming committed”

    During a partition an old leader may continue acting on stale authority while a majority elects a newer-term leader. Consensus safety depends on terms, quorum intersection, and commit rules—not on a mystical guarantee that only one process ever thinks it leads.

    Try it: Partition a five-node cluster into 2+3 and explain which side can create a new committed entry and why the minority’s old leader cannot.

    Part III — Naming and Replicated State

    Section titled “Part III — Naming and Replicated State”

    Chapter 8 — Naming, Discovery, and Decentralized Lookup

    Section titled “Chapter 8 — Naming, Discovery, and Decentralized Lookup”

    A healthy service can be unreachable because discovery is stale—and an unhealthy service can remain discoverable

    Section titled “A healthy service can be unreachable because discovery is stale—and an unhealthy service can remain discoverable”

    Naming and discovery systems publish beliefs about where services live. Leases, caches, and health checks make those beliefs time-dependent, so registry state and real process state are rarely updated at the same instant.

    Try it: Write a timeline from worker crash to lease expiry to client refresh and mark every interval where registry truth and physical truth differ.

    Chapter 9 — Replication and Consistency

    Section titled “Chapter 9 — Replication and Consistency”

    Adding replicas can make writes less available if acknowledgement requires too many of them

    Section titled “Adding replicas can make writes less available if acknowledgement requires too many of them”

    Replication creates redundancy, but the protocol decides whether that redundancy helps availability. A write that waits for every replica can block on one slow copy even though other copies are healthy.

    Try it: Compare write availability for a three-replica system requiring 1, 2, or 3 acknowledgements under one replica failure.

    A CRDT can converge perfectly to a state that violates the business rule

    Section titled “A CRDT can converge perfectly to a state that violates the business rule”

    Convergence means replicas deterministically reach equivalent state after receiving the same information. It does not guarantee constraints such as inventory never becoming negative or a username being globally unique without additional design or coordination.

    Try it: Design two disconnected inventory updates that merge deterministically but violate a nonnegative-stock invariant.

    Part IV — Fault Tolerance

    Section titled “Part IV — Fault Tolerance”

    Chapter 10 — Fault Tolerance I — Failure Models, Resilience, and Recovery

    Section titled “Chapter 10 — Fault Tolerance I — Failure Models, Resilience, and Recovery”

    A perfect crash detector would need to know whether a silent node is dead or merely slow

    Section titled “A perfect crash detector would need to know whether a silent node is dead or merely slow”

    In an asynchronous setting, a sufficiently delayed healthy process can look exactly like a crashed process from another node’s local perspective. Practical detectors therefore produce suspicions under timing assumptions rather than omniscient truth.

    Try it: Run the same heartbeat timeout once with a killed process and once with injected delay. Compare the detector’s local evidence.

    Consensus can succeed while the client still has no idea whether its operation happened

    Section titled “Consensus can succeed while the client still has no idea whether its operation happened”

    A command may be committed and applied before the leader crashes or the response is lost. Replicated agreement solves ordering among servers; request identity and deduplication solve the client’s retry ambiguity.

    Try it: Trace a request that commits, loses its response, changes leaders, and retries. Mark which state must survive for one logical effect.

    Chapter 11 — Fault Tolerance II — Transactions, Recovery, and Distributed State

    Section titled “Chapter 11 — Fault Tolerance II — Transactions, Recovery, and Distributed State”

    The cheapest distributed transaction is often the one the data model avoids

    Section titled “The cheapest distributed transaction is often the one the data model avoids”

    Operations within one shard can use one local authority, while cross-shard invariants require communication, durable transaction state, and failure recovery. Good partitioning can remove coordination from the common path rather than merely optimize it later.

    Try it: Take five application operations and choose a partition key that maximizes the fraction that remain single-shard.

    Part V — Security and Operations

    Section titled “Part V — Security and Operations”

    Chapter 12 — Security, Trust, and Operating Distributed Systems

    Section titled “Chapter 12 — Security, Trust, and Operating Distributed Systems”

    Four replicas may be needed to tolerate one Byzantine fault where three were enough for one crash fault

    Section titled “Four replicas may be needed to tolerate one Byzantine fault where three were enough for one crash fault”

    Stronger adversary models require stronger redundancy and quorum reasoning. A malicious node can equivocate rather than merely disappear, so a crash-fault majority protocol cannot simply be relabeled Byzantine tolerant.

    Try it: Use n >= 3f + 1 for f = 1 and compare the resulting four-replica requirement with a three-node Raft cluster.

    An AI agent’s instruction is not an authorization boundary

    Section titled “An AI agent’s instruction is not an authorization boundary”

    If an agent can invoke a tool, the tool service must enforce identity, scopes, validation, idempotency, and audit rules independently of the model prompt. Natural-language intent is not a substitute for a security control.

    Try it: Design a calendar tool where the agent can read events but cannot send invitations, and identify where that restriction must be enforced.

    Retries can turn a temporary slowdown into a self-sustaining outage

    Section titled “Retries can turn a temporary slowdown into a self-sustaining outage”

    Latency causes timeouts; timeouts cause retries; retries add work; added work grows queues and latency. A mechanism intended to improve reliability can create positive feedback unless bounded by backoff, jitter, budgets, or admission control.

    Try it: Draw the retry-amplification loop and identify at least three places where a control can break the feedback cycle.
