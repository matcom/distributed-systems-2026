Syllabus

What you are building toward

Section titled “What you are building toward”

Distributed systems begin when one program can no longer assume one memory, one scheduler, one clock, one failure boundary, or one perfectly shared view of state.

Keep this question in view:

How do you build a useful service when machines, networks, clocks, software, and operators can fail independently?

You will implement, break, measure, and explain distributed services while learning the models and algorithms needed to reason about them.

What you should be able to do

Section titled “What you should be able to do”

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

  1. explain why latency, concurrency, independent state, and partial failure change the meaning of a remote operation;
  2. compare distributed architectures and identify which responsibilities middleware provides;
  3. distinguish thread, process, container, VM, host, and network failure boundaries;
  4. design RPC/message interfaces with explicit deadlines, retries, request identity, and duplicate semantics;
  5. choose among direct messaging, dissemination, gossip, multicast-style, and overlay communication patterns;
  6. reason about physical-clock uncertainty, happened-before, Lamport clocks, vector clocks, and concurrent events;
  7. distinguish mutual exclusion, leader election, quorum coordination, and consensus;
  8. separate names, identifiers, and addresses and design discovery/bootstrap mechanisms that tolerate change;
  9. compare replication strategies and consistency guarantees using concrete observable histories;
  10. state a failure model and test crashes, delay, loss, partitions, false suspicion, overload, and recovery;
  11. reason about persistence, recovery, transactions, sharding, ownership movement, and cross-owner atomicity;
  12. define trust boundaries, authentication/authorization rules, replay behavior, and the consequences of Byzantine participants;
  13. use logs, metrics, traces, state dumps, and controlled workloads to support or reject a systems claim;
  14. defend your design from reproducible evidence rather than from successful screenshots or framework defaults.

Course structure

Section titled “Course structure”

The required course has one Warm-up and 12 chapters. The order follows Tanenbaum and van Steen:

  1. Thinking in Distributed Systems
  2. Architectures and Middleware
  3. Processes, Virtualization, and Distributed Execution
  4. Communication I — Messages, RPC, and Remote Failure
  5. Communication II — Messaging, Dissemination, and Overlays
  6. Coordination I — Time, Events, and Causality
  7. Coordination II — Mutual Exclusion, Elections, and Agreement
  8. Naming, Discovery, and Decentralized Lookup
  9. Replication and Consistency
  10. Fault Tolerance I — Failure Models, Resilience, and Recovery
  11. Fault Tolerance II — Transactions, Recovery, and Distributed State
  12. Security, Trust, and Operating Distributed Systems

How you study each chapter

Section titled “How you study each chapter”

Use the same rhythm throughout the course:

Understand → Predict → Worked example → Guided practice → Knowledge check → Main laboratory → Test/Measure/Diagnose → Explain → Optional extension

Do not begin with commands. State what you expect and why. After execution, identify what the evidence actually establishes and what remains uncertain.

Guided practice is a practice bank, not a requirement to rush through every prompt in one sitting. Work enough cases to meet the chapter’s Practice target without notes. Return to the remaining cases for spaced retrieval, comparison, or instructor-assigned work. The knowledge check tells you where to revisit the lesson before entering the larger lab.

Each chapter begins with a short Before you read prediction and identifies the prior knowledge you should bring forward. At the end of the lab, return to that prediction and state what changed in your explanation.

Distance-learning and bandwidth assumptions

Section titled “Distance-learning and bandwidth assumptions”

The required path is local-first and offline-first.

  • The written chapter is the authoritative lesson.
  • Mandatory videos do not exist.
  • Every required lab runs on one ordinary Linux-capable computer.
  • Paid cloud infrastructure, paid SaaS products, and paid AI APIs are not prerequisites.
  • Local processes are preferred until a stronger isolation or network boundary is needed.
  • Docker/Podman or namespaces may be used when available, with local-process fallbacks where practical.
  • All deterministic tests are visible and runnable without GitHub.
  • External papers and systems are extensions; the course explanation remains sufficient when connectivity is poor.
  • The offline course bundles provide the static reading, chapter source materials, runnable lab starter code, reports, public tests, and local course tooling. Third-party compilers, runtimes, packages, and operating-system utilities must be prepared on the machine before disconnecting; use the Course Tools & Offline Environment page as the installation checklist.
  • Submit compact source, logs, histories, measurements, and text explanations rather than large screen recordings.

A useful workflow is simply:

download
   ↓
disconnect
   ↓
read / predict / build / break / measure / explain
   ↓
reconnect and synchronize

Practical environment

Section titled “Practical environment”

The normal toolset stays small and inspectable:

  • Linux;
  • Git;
  • Go for native services;
  • Python for introductory Ray work and small analysis/support tools;
  • HTTP/gRPC/Protocol Buffers where they clarify communication semantics;
  • curl, grpcurl, ss, tcpdump, and Wireshark when packet/endpoint evidence is useful;
  • go test, the race detector, and profiling tools;
  • process kill/restart, local fault wrappers, namespaces/containers, and tc netem where available;
  • structured logs, metrics, and trace/correlation identifiers.

The Toolbox gives task-oriented guidance without making any hosted observability platform mandatory. The complete installation/offline checklist is maintained on Course Tools & Offline Environment.

Bilingual technical terminology

Section titled “Bilingual technical terminology”

The English and Spanish editions must preserve the same technical distinctions even when the vocabulary differs. In Spanish, prefer a Spanish explanation and keep the common English systems term when it is the identifier students will see in code, papers, APIs, or professional documentation. Terms such as safety, liveness, log, membership, retry, and peer should be introduced consistently rather than replaced by different synonyms from chapter to chapter.

Ray’s role

Section titled “Ray’s role”

Use Ray early to experience a productive distributed runtime and to see how much machinery a framework can hide.

Then make the hidden decisions explicit.

Ray is useful as a temporary scaffold early in the course. It is not the architecture you must preserve. When you remove it, preserve the external behavior you need and implement only the communication, state, discovery, coordination, recovery, and observability mechanisms your design actually requires.

One evolving practical system

Section titled “One evolving practical system”

The chapter labs intentionally connect. A simple service first crosses a process boundary, then gains explicit communication, dissemination, coordination, discovery, replication, recovery, multi-owner state, and trust boundaries.

You do not need to keep every mechanism forever. Replacing a mechanism with a better fit is part of the work.

Testing and evidence

Section titled “Testing and evidence”

There are no hidden deterministic correctness tests. Inspect and run all official tests locally, extend them, and use them as one source of evidence.

Some distributed properties cannot be established by ordinary tests alone. Use controlled histories, fault injection, persistent state, measurements, and explicit invariants when the claim depends on message order, timing, recovery, or workload.

A useful report structure is:

claim
prediction
controlled scenario
configuration
evidence
observed result
conclusion
remaining uncertainty

AI-assisted engineering

Section titled “AI-assisted engineering”

You may use AI assistants and coding agents unless an activity explicitly marks an AI-independent checkpoint.

Treat generated output as a hypothesis, patch, or design proposal—not as proof. You remain responsible for reviewing it, testing it, explaining it, and modifying it during technical review.

Assessment alignment

Section titled “Assessment alignment”

Assessment is organized around the same evidence you produce while learning:

Capability Where you learn it Evidence that demonstrates it
reason about remote uncertainty Chapters 1, 4, 10 timeout histories, retry predictions, failure-model explanations
allocate architecture responsibilities Chapters 2, 3, 8 architecture/dependency maps, failure-domain and discovery analysis
choose communication semantics Chapters 4–5 RPC/redelivery traces, idempotency evidence, dissemination/overlay experiments
reason about order and coordination Chapters 6–7 causal traces, mutual-exclusion/election comparisons, Raft invariants
reason about replicated state Chapters 9–10 consistency histories, quorum experiments, CRDT convergence, crash/replay recovery
coordinate multiple owners Chapter 11 2PC/recovery traces, compensation analysis, ownership movement
operate and defend a service Chapter 12 latency/backpressure diagnosis, trust model, auth/replay evidence, Byzantine analysis

Assessment prioritizes observable systems skill: correct reasoning, implementation, failure/recovery behavior, experiment design, consistency guarantees, debugging evidence, performance interpretation, reproducibility, and individual explanation.

A screenshot that says “success” is supporting evidence. It is not a distributed-systems argument.
